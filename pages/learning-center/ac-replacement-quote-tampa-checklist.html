7 Questions to Ask Before Signing an AC Replacement Quote in Tampa

September 1, 2026 • 10 min read • Reviewed by Dustin Miller

Quick Answer

A trustworthy Tampa AC replacement quote should identify the exact indoor and outdoor equipment, explain how the system was sized, show the efficiency rating and AHRI match, state what labor and materials are included, address permits and inspections, and put every warranty in writing. Do not compare proposals by total price alone; compare the complete scope.

Replacing an air conditioner is one of the larger purchases a Tampa homeowner will make, and two proposals that look similar on the first page can represent very different installations. One may include properly matched equipment, duct and airflow evaluation, permits, start-up testing, and written labor coverage. Another may show only a brand name, tonnage, and a total.

That difference matters in North Tampa. Our long cooling season places sustained demand on equipment, while attic heat, humidity, drainage, duct leakage, and installation quality all affect comfort. ENERGY STAR warns that improper HVAC installation can reduce efficiency by as much as 30% and may shorten equipment life.[1]

At On The Way Heating & Air, complete replacement systems start at $8,000 and increase based on the home’s requirements and the homeowner’s comfort, efficiency, and equipment preferences. A responsible proposal should explain why the recommended option costs what it does. Before you sign an AC replacement quote in Tampa, ask the following seven questions.

1. What Exact Equipment Is Included?

A proposal should identify more than a brand and a tonnage. Ask for the complete model numbers for the outdoor unit, indoor air handler or furnace, and evaporator coil. If the proposal includes a thermostat, heat kit, air-quality accessory, surge protection, or another component, that item should also be identified.

The indoor and outdoor components need to operate as a matched system. The Air-Conditioning, Heating, and Refrigeration Institute maintains the AHRI Directory, where consumers and contractors can verify certified product performance by product line, rating, or brand.[2] Ask the contractor for the AHRI reference number or certificate for the proposed combination.

2. How Did You Determine the System Size?

Replacing an old three-ton unit with another three-ton unit is not, by itself, a sizing calculation. The old equipment could have been oversized, the home may have received new windows or insulation, an addition may have changed the load, or ductwork problems may be limiting airflow.

ENERGY STAR advises homeowners to expect a home evaluation and says equipment should be sized using the home’s actual characteristics—including square footage, insulation, windows, ducts, and airflow—rather than a rule of thumb.[3] Oversized equipment can cycle too frequently, reducing humidity removal and comfort while potentially shortening equipment life.[4]

Ask what information was used to determine capacity, whether windows and insulation were considered, whether the existing duct system was evaluated, and why the recommended size differs from the old system if a change is proposed. For Tampa homes, sizing is also a humidity-control decision. A system that drops the temperature too quickly may not run long enough to remove moisture effectively.

3. What Is Included in the Installed Price—and What Is Not?

The total should be supported by an itemized scope. ENERGY STAR specifically recommends written, itemized estimates and a written proposal that includes project cost, model numbers, schedule, and warranty information.[3]

A complete Tampa AC installation proposal may address removal and disposal of old equipment, refrigerant recovery, equipment pads or stands, drain-line work, float switches, electrical disconnects, breakers or wiring, line-set reuse or replacement, thermostat work, permit fees, inspection coordination, start-up testing, and corrections to accessible duct connections. Not every home needs every item, but exclusions should be clear before work begins.

Price-comparison rule: Compare scope to scope—not total to total. A lower proposal may simply exclude work that appears later as a change order.

Ask for written answers if the quote uses phrases such as “as needed,” “standard installation,” or “existing materials reused.” Those phrases are not necessarily a problem, but they should be defined.

4. Are Permits, Inspections, and Required Documents Included?

Permit requirements depend on the property’s jurisdiction and the scope of work. A home with a Tampa mailing address may fall under the City of Tampa, unincorporated Hillsborough County, Pasco County, or another authority. Ask who will obtain the mechanical permit, whether the fee is included, and who will coordinate the final inspection.

Hillsborough County’s published resources specifically discuss mechanical permits for the repair or replacement of heating and air-conditioning systems. The county also states that a Notice of Commencement is required for a mechanical-only project when the cost is $15,000 or more.[5] A Notice of Commencement threshold is not the same thing as a permit exemption, so confirm the requirements for your exact address and project.

You should also verify the contractor’s Florida license through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation. Florida’s official portal allows searches by name, license number, city, county, or license type.[6] On The Way Heating & Air operates under Florida license CAC1820863.

5. What Efficiency and Comfort Result Should I Expect?

SEER2 is a seasonal cooling-efficiency rating, but it is not a promise of a specific electric bill. Ask the contractor to explain the proposed system’s certified SEER2 rating, whether it uses single-stage, two-stage, or variable-capacity operation, and how those features relate to your goals.

The U.S. Department of Energy classifies Florida as a hot-humid/Southeast state. In DOE’s standardized example, a 15.2-SEER2 ENERGY STAR system uses less annual cooling energy than a 13.4-SEER2 system, while a 23.5-SEER2 system uses less again.[7] Your actual savings will depend on runtime, thermostat settings, duct condition, insulation, home size, utility rates, and installation quality.

6. What Warranties Are in Writing—and Who Is Responsible for Them?

“Ten-year warranty” is incomplete. Determine whether the proposal is referring to a manufacturer parts warranty, compressor warranty, labor warranty, or all three. Ask whether registration is required, who completes it, what maintenance conditions apply, whether diagnostic charges are covered, and whether labor coverage is provided by the installing contractor or a separate warranty administrator.

The proposal should state manufacturer parts coverage, compressor or heat-exchanger coverage if different, labor warranty length and exclusions, workmanship coverage, who handles future warranty calls, and whether maintenance records are required.

This is also where company ownership and accountability matter. A private-equity-owned company, franchise, or large regional operator is not automatically a poor choice, and a local contractor is not automatically a good one. The practical question is: Who will stand behind this installation after the salesperson leaves?

7. Am I Being Given Time and Information to Make a Decision?

Urgency can be real when an AC fails in August, but urgency should not eliminate transparency. Be cautious when a large discount exists only if you sign immediately, when financing is discussed before the equipment and scope, or when the contractor will not leave a written proposal.

Before authorizing work, you should understand what failed in the current system, whether repair remains practical, why replacement is recommended, what equipment and work are included, what could change the final price, and what happens if the system does not meet the agreed comfort expectations.

Older equipment deserves an honest explanation as well. EPA states that existing R-22 systems may still be serviced; the equipment is not illegal. However, new R-22 production and import ended in 2020, so supply is limited to reclaimed or previously produced refrigerant and price and availability may change.[8] That information should be used to explain repair economics—not to frighten a homeowner into signing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should be included in an AC replacement quote in Tampa?

The quote should identify exact equipment model numbers, capacity, SEER2 rating, AHRI match, labor and materials, exclusions, permits, start-up testing, schedule, total price, and manufacturer and labor warranties. It should also explain how the contractor determined the correct system size.

How much does an AC replacement cost in Tampa?

At On The Way Heating & Air, complete systems start at $8,000. Final pricing depends on capacity, efficiency, home conditions, required duct or electrical work, accessories, and installation scope. Homeowners should compare itemized scopes rather than total prices alone.

Is the lowest AC replacement quote the best choice?

Not necessarily. A lower total may exclude permits, electrical work, drain protection, duct corrections, thermostat work, labor coverage, or start-up verification. Compare equipment and scope line by line.

Can an R-22 air conditioner still be repaired?

Yes. EPA says existing R-22 equipment may still be serviced. New R-22 production and import ended in 2020, so reclaimed and previously produced refrigerant supply, price, and availability can affect whether a major repair is economically practical.[8]

How can I verify a Florida HVAC contractor’s license?

Use the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation’s official license-search portal. You can search by name, license number, city, county, or license type.[6]
